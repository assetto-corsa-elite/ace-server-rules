# ACE editorial direction

This is the editorial standard for future Automatic Result Post production. Apply it alongside PRODUCTION.md and ANNOUNCEMENTS.md.

## Give each stage its own job

| Stage | Editorial purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Practice Highlights | Reveal preparation, emerging pace, model comparisons and useful team form. State the snapshot cutoff. |
| Forecast | Identify concrete unresolved questions using historical form, practice and championship stakes. |
| Pre-Race Briefing | Make the race easy to prepare for. Prioritize actionable settings, schedule and essential context. |
| Qualifying Report | Explain the grid, meaningful gaps and what preparation translated into performance. |
| Race Results | Tell the decisive individual and collective story of the finishing order. |
| Race Insights | Explain how the race developed using lap data, gaps, consistency and verified observations. |
| Standings | Explain what changed in both championships and which gaps now matter. |
| Next-Round Forecast | Carry the consequences forward into specific questions for the next circuit. |
| Announcements | Give the outcome or essentials quickly and encourage opening the full Results sequence. |

Repeating a number is justified when it serves a new purpose. For example, the winning margin describes the finish in Results; its evolution over laps can support an explanation in Insights. Remove repeated paragraphs and interchangeable transitions.

## Find the round's central story

Choose one supported thread: a comeback, reversal, rivalry, team result, tight contest or change in championship order. Use two or three decisive facts to develop it. Give detailed tables their place without making the narrative read like a table recited aloud.

Track storylines across rounds. Explain whether the latest result confirms, changes or complicates the earlier picture. Fresh evidence must justify recurring labels such as favourite, recovery or improvement.

## Cover the whole field

Look beyond the podium for meaningful progress, midfield contests, first points, position gains and personal milestones. Select stories because their evidence is interesting. Rotate attention naturally across the season.

Compare improvement on the same circuit/layout and comparable conditions when possible. Identify material differences in car, ballast, track limits or session conditions. A low finishing position alone does not establish poor driving.

## Give teams a real story

When relevant, show the round's team points, both drivers' contributions and consequences for the championship. Identify the second driver's impact, changes in the scoring balance and meaningful teammate gaps.

Include team content in forecasts and relevant posters. A team section should explain a consequence or contest, supported by the numbers. Use existing team membership at the relevant round and flag membership changes when they affect the comparison.

## Put statistics in context

Report what the metric measures, with useful sample context. Consistency over three laps and consistency over a full race need different interpretation. Separate completed-lap observations from within-lap events. Distinguish cumulative lap-time gaps from official finishing gaps.

Explain an incident, pit strategy, retirement cause or overtake only when the available data supports that explanation. A visible change on a graph supports an observation; causal language needs additional evidence.

## Caustic, surprising humour

Strengthen the humour. Use sharp understatement, unexpected comparisons, abrupt punchlines, dry absurdity and occasional affectionate roasting grounded in a specific race fact. Vary the rhythm and subject so the joke does not feel generated from the same formula each week.

Keep the sporting fact immediately legible. Let a sharp line land after a result or contrast. Humour may be pointed and irreverent. Keep insults focused on a racing situation, an absurd outcome or the narrator's framing. Avoid invented motives, unsupported blame, private circumstances and personal vulnerabilities.

Use humour selectively. Some sections benefit from one strong line; others need straight reporting. There is no joke quota. Protect the clarity of the briefing and the neutrality of race-review decisions. Preserve the organizer's penalty wording accurately.

These English examples illustrate the register using the archived Nordschleife facts. They are examples for tone, not stock lines to reuse in future rounds:

- Versthappening, P16 to P3: “Thirteen places gained. The midfield has requested that he start from the car park next time.”
- Kaasglenneman, P3 on the grid and three completed laps: “Qualified third. Completed three laps. A deeply unhelpful commitment to the number.”

Discard any joke that could be pasted unchanged beneath almost any race result. Avoid repeatedly reaching for spreadsheets, tax bills, missing earphones, therapy or the same gaming reference. Reuse a community callback only when a new event gives it a fresh payoff. Read the recent published packs before selecting callbacks so the humour stays unpredictable.

## Preserve detail through structure

Information volume may remain substantial. Use short sentences, one clear idea per line and blank lines between sections. Bullet individual performances and team contributions. Keep indentation shallow. Avoid tabs and four-space paragraph indents that produce Discord code blocks.

When a detailed post exceeds Discord's message limit, split it at a logical section boundary, retaining the information and publication order. Only the authorized first message receives everyone. Native driver mentions and checked emoji remain resolved through the central mappings.

## Poster composition

Give each poster a dominant visual or factual focus, with a readable secondary hierarchy. Use actual track imagery and retrieved server liveries. Exact results and full-field comparisons retain their tables and clear labels. Maintain the ACE palette, logo and factual detail.

The narrative and visuals should complement one another. Put the team stakes visibly on the forecast poster. Keep driver names, units, data sources and chart methods legible at the delivered size.

## Editorial check before release

- Can the reader identify this round's main story from the opening section?
- Does each stage contribute a distinct explanation or consequence?
- Are both individual and team performances represented where relevant?
- Has a meaningful story outside the podium been considered?
- Are sample sizes and material comparison differences clear?
- Does each joke grow from a real fact, and is its construction fresh relative to recent packs?
- Can the text be scanned through short sentences, line breaks and lists without losing detail?
- Do the linked posters show the promised driver and team information?

These editorial checks supplement the automated integrity and syntax tests. Human-readable narrative quality is reviewed from the finished text and posters.
